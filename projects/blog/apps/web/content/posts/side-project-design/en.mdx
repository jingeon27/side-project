</Summary>

I wanted to turn ideas I cared about into services. I am building a photo community, Game Mate, and a personal blog. I ask AI to implement things, try the resulting screens myself, and send back changes.

The first project was `photo-contest`. The idea came from a streamer's segment called a “Yeongdo contest.” It made me think it would be fun to have a service where close friends could share funny photos within their own group.

I started with inviting friends to a group, submitting photos, and voting. It had separate submission and voting periods, with rules for ties and contests without votes.

## I came to see photos and got a group list

The first home screen was mostly about choosing a group. I wanted the latest photos from my groups instead. I moved the group list to My Page and made photos the first thing on the home screen.

I also wanted a place that did not close when a contest ended. I added ongoing posts without a submission period, including videos and edited images alongside photos. Administrators could remove posts and see the month's popular posts too.

Running the screens brought up design changes. I changed layouts that covered photos and removed a story-style border when there was no story feature. I checked spacing and layout on desktop separately.

The entry screen brings login, signup, and “Start anonymously” together. Anonymous sessions can browse public groups. Private groups require login and membership approval.

Preserving anonymity was part of the plan too. I chose not to add social login so people could use the service without linking an external social account.

## Game Mate starts with Clash Royale

Game Mate grew out of watching Clash Royale communities. The overseas players I saw used Twitter, while Korean players used KakaoTalk, DC Inside, and Naver Cafe.

Getting added as a friend by top-ranked players overseas was difficult, and I also came across posts looking for someone to teach the game. Posting across several platforms and checking each one looked inconvenient. That made me consider building a service where people could make these requests and find each other in one place.

I also wanted someone stuck in a game to be able to describe their situation and receive advice from other players. I chose Clash Royale first because I thought I could reach its initial users through communities I knew.

I considered Brawl Stars and League of Legends too, but decided against supporting several games at the start. I made five boards under Clash Royale: feedback, friend-add sales, friendly matches, coaching, and general discussion. More games could use those boards later. I left account trading out because of the risk.

Friend-add sales have a price field. Coaching posts can list hourly, monthly, or target-rating fees, and either students or coaches can post. People can read the terms and talk through chat. I decided not to add payments inside the service.

I kept the photo service and Game Mate's accounts and data separate. Both involve other people's opinions, but that did not give people viewing photos and people asking for game advice a reason to share an account.

## Who would answer the first question?

I was adding game profiles and multiple languages, but the first question still needed someone to answer it. A question left unanswered gives its author little reason to come back.

```text
Someone posts a question
  → Another player replies
  → The requester receives a notification
  → The requester reads it and marks whether it helped
```

I had initially thought as far as saving questions and replies. I also needed to tell the requester that a reply had arrived and get them back to read it. That is why I added reply notifications and a `Helpful` action.

First, I want to see how many requests receive at least one reply marked helpful. Several helpful replies on one request still count as one request. I also want to look at time to the first reply and the proportion answered within 24 hours.

I plan to find five to ten people willing to answer and manually connect them with questions in existing communities. I have not started recruiting yet.

## Looking up a game profile did not prove ownership

Connecting a game account starts with entering a Player Tag and loading the profile. But someone can enter another player's tag. A successful lookup should not produce an ownership verification label.

I decided connected profiles should start with ownership unverified. I also treated administrator-managed user verification separately from ownership of a game account. I did not want the screen to call something verified when we had not checked it.

The early plan included saving a snapshot of the deck and game information with each post. During implementation, I moved game profiles to the user account and removed deck attachments from the writing screen. Profile verification requests now start a one-to-one chat with an administrator.

## Things people do not have to do during signup

Connecting a game profile is optional at signup. People who want to connect immediately can look up their tag. Others can join with a nickname and the required agreements, then connect later.

As the form grew, I made the body scroll while keeping the completion button at the bottom. I checked short viewports to make sure the inputs and button did not overlap.

I also disliked editing a profile through an expandable section. I changed it to an Edit Profile button that opens a Bottom Sheet, with the nickname and bio in one place. Web addresses in the bio became clickable on the profile too.

## If the post is translated, what about the reply?

I started Game Mate with Korean, English, and Japanese in mind. At first, translating menus and posts seemed enough.

But a Japanese-speaking player could read a translated Korean question and answer in Japanese. The person who asked might then be unable to read the reply. Replies needed translation too.

Language routes are in place, but translation features and running the service for each language still need work. The mobile app uses the web screens through a WebView. I want to refine asking and reading replies on the web first, then deal with app distribution and the stores.

## The blog's about page did not need my resume too

I originally kept my blog posts in a separate repository. Moving them over to Tistory to publish was cumbersome. Building a blog of my own had also become less expensive, so I decided to make one.

During implementation, the AI agent went beyond what I had asked for and built a resume too. I removed it and kept only contact information on the about page.

Korean and English editions only appear where an actual translation exists.

I am still trying the screens and revising them. For Game Mate, I need to find people who will ask and people who will answer. I want to see whether someone receives a reply and comes back to read it.
