<Summary>

A question about success, failure, and effort made me think about another criterion: whether today's effort makes the next task easier.

</Summary>

I was asked to rank four experiences by their value.

1. Success without effort
2. Failure without effort
3. Success with effort
4. Failure with effort

I chose 3 → 4 → 1 → 2. I put the two experiences involving effort first. Of the others, success might leave a process I could examine and reuse; I assumed failure without effort would leave little to learn.

The question stayed with me. Should doing nothing and achieving something with little effort mean the same thing?

## Replace the presence of effort with its amount

At the time, success without effort made me think of luck, and failure without effort seemed to offer little learning. If the question is about commitment to work, I would still put the experiences involving effort first.

But measuring the amount of effort changes the choices.

1. Success with little effort
2. Failure with little effort
3. Success with much effort
4. Failure with much effort

When exploring new ways of working, I would lean toward 1 → 2 → 3 → 4.

## Reduce the work needed for the same result

Experience and automation can make success take less effort. Reusing code and verification steps from earlier work reduces the need to explain and check everything from scratch.

Achieving the same quality with less work leaves time for another problem. That is the workflow I want to develop.

## What a small failure can teach

I first dismissed the second choice as yielding nothing. A small attempt that tests an assumption can still leave something useful.

A short implementation may reveal that an approach does not fit. Knowing why it failed, with little to undo, makes another approach easier to try.

For evaluating completed work, I would value a successful outcome more. Here I am considering exploration: several short attempts can sometimes help the next decision more than one approach pursued for a long time.

## My experience with coding agents followed a similar pattern

At first, I repeatedly wrote long prompts and discarded the output. Later, I could get the intended result, but still had to read all the code and repeat the same corrections.

Recording decisions in skills, tests, and lint gradually changed that. I had a clearer place to resume when work went wrong and fewer standards to explain again on the next task.

Small features and refactorings sometimes meet my expectations without edits from me. I still read and verify them, but no longer have to finish every implementation by correcting it myself.

This is not a sequence every task must follow. In my experience, earlier effort has increasingly made later work easier.

## Check what today's effort leaves for the next task

I try not to judge work solely by how long I spent on it. I also ask whether the next similar task will need the same explanations and corrections.

I want to preserve decisions in code and verification standards so the next task takes less work. Looking back on success or failure now includes asking what it left for the next attempt.
