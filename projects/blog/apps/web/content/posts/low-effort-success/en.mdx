Someone asked me to rank four experiences by how valuable they were.

1. Succeeding without effort
2. Failing without effort
3. Succeeding with effort
4. Failing with effort

I chose 3 → 4 → 1 → 2. I put 3 and 4 first because both involved effort. Of the remaining two, I placed 2 last because nothing was gained. I put 1 before it because reflecting on a success could provide a foundation for the next attempt.

Something still bothered me after answering: the axis of the question.

## Replace whether effort exists with how much it costs

The original question divides effort into present or absent. In that framing, effortless success looks like luck, and effortless failure looks like nothing at all. The order 3-4-1-2 seems right. If the question is about attitude, I would still answer that way.

But replacing the presence of effort with its amount changes the choices.

1. Succeeding with little effort
2. Failing with little effort
3. Succeeding with much effort
4. Failing with much effort

My ranking reverses: **1 → 2 → 3 → 4.**

## If the same result can cost less

“Succeeding without effort” sounds like luck. It is difficult to value something that cannot be reproduced.

“Succeeding with little effort” can be the result of systems, skill, and automation. If a success costs less, it can be repeated more often, leaving capacity for the next problem. Its value includes the cost of producing each success.

That is why I put 1 first. It is the state I want to reach.

## The biggest change is the value of 2

In my original answer, I dismissed 2 as gaining nothing. That judgment holds only when failure truly involved no effort.

A failure with little effort can be a cheap, quick experiment. Lowering the cost of one failure makes more attempts possible, and more attempts can accelerate learning. Repeated failures at low cost are what keep that learning process moving.

Putting a low-effort failure above a high-effort success is the least intuitive part of this ranking. It makes sense when the criterion moves from the outcome of one round to learning per unit of cost. Ten inexpensive losses can teach more than one large win.

## The path runs from 4 back to 1

The order 1-2-3-4 describes value, not the order of arrival. The path goes the other way: **4 → 3 → 2 → 1.**

Learning to work with AI followed that path for me.

I started with high-effort failures, at 4. I supplied whole blocks of context, rewrote long prompts, and repeatedly discarded the entire result. Those expensive failures taught me what did not work.

They accumulated into high-effort success, at 3. The work could succeed, but a person still had to guide it closely every time. Staying there means paying that same cost for every success.

The next step was to record recurring decisions outside my head: document rules, divide context by area, and automate checks. Failure became cheaper, at 2. Finding and correcting each mistake took less effort, which allowed more attempts in the same amount of time.

After enough of those inexpensive failures, low-effort success becomes possible, at 1. From outside, it can look as though the work simply succeeded without much effort.

The ranking 1-2-3-4 describes both the order of value and the journey viewed backward from its destination.

## Little effort still has a cost

I do not mean that effort is unnecessary.

The low cost of 1 comes from work already paid for in 4 and 3. Without those earlier expensive failures, there is no system for failing cheaply. Trying to skip straight to 1 returns to the original idea of relying on luck.

My two answers therefore do not conflict. For attitude, I choose 3-4-1-2 because effort comes first. For direction, I choose 1-2-3-4 because I want today's work to reduce the cost of tomorrow's work.

I try to ask more than whether a task finished successfully. Next time I do something similar, will I repeat the same explanations and corrections? If today's effort lowers the cost of the next attempt, both success and failure can leave something useful behind.
