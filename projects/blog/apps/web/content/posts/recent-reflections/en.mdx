This post covers what I did not know and what I am studying now.

I had a 30-minute video interview and could not answer most of the questions well. When I went back over them afterward, the questions were not unusually hard. There was simply a lot I did not know. Even things I thought I knew fell apart once I tried to explain them.

I got stuck the most on questions about Yarn Workspaces and testing. After the interview, I studied both topics again and wrote them up as posts on [Yarn Workspaces and Turborepo](/en/blog/yarn-workspaces-turborepo-build-graph-and-cache) and on [testing infinite scroll and dropdown menus](/en/blog/testing-infinite-scroll-and-dropdown-states).

## I thought this time would be different

At my first company, I built the frontend of a recruiting SaaS. Reading the source of several open source libraries taught me to write library-level type inference and component designs. The CEO, a developer by background, valued my work, so when I said I was leaving, the CEO asked whether I would keep working for the company as a freelancer from home.

After leaving, I burned out a little. I rested for a while, then felt I had to do something, so I joined a team project called Doneness on BeSide. It was a cooking timer that also showed tips for getting food to the doneness you want. During those six months off, I applied for the first time to the company I have now interviewed with again. My development instincts had dulled, and in the interview I could not answer most of the questions, including basic syntax questions.

Then I joined my current company, and at first I lost the team's trust. The approaches from my previous job looked good to me, so I persuaded the team to switch our form and UI libraries. I did not properly track the side effects of those changes, and they caused a lot of bugs. For the next three months, I focused on finishing small tasks without mistakes to rebuild that trust.

Once I had it back, I introduced testing, monitoring, and a way of working with coding agents one by one. Now I am the only frontend developer, building the user-facing service, the hospital admin, and the back office, and our pace has actually picked up. I even heard people on the development team call me the ace.

A year and a half later, I interviewed with that company again. Before the interview, I went back over my past work and prepared the questions and answers that work was likely to raise. Along with basics such as TypeScript, I reviewed these topics again.

- Browser rendering: the order from the DOM and CSSOM through style calculation, layout, paint, rasterization, and compositing, and which step a given change sends the browser back to
- The event loop: when tasks, microtasks, and `requestAnimationFrame` callbacks run, and why long tasks block input and screen updates
- The browser back button and the History API, React's render and commit phases, cookies and CORS

I thought it would go differently. The questions I got were not the ones I had expected. I do not have the result yet, but the interview itself went the same way as the first one.

## I had not thought the answers through

The questions started from my own work and kept turning into "Why did you do it that way?" They needed real thought, and since they were not the questions I had expected, I had not thought them through in advance.

At work, when I ran into a problem, I took the time to look things up and read the code before deciding. In the interview, I had to answer questions that deserved the same depth of thought in a short time, and words came out while I was still organizing my thoughts. Even as I spoke, I was not sure I was answering the question.

Testing was the same. At work I had kept tests for a new feature's expected behavior separate from tests that reproduce bugs found in production ([the standards I set when introducing tests](/en/blog/testing-standards)), but when asked why I split them that way, I could not explain it in my own words.

## Even what I had studied in depth had faded

Some technologies I studied in depth, and others only as far as I needed. Monorepos were in the first group: I looked into them quite thoroughly when I first introduced one. But studying something in depth did not mean it stayed with me. What I learned back then never made it into long-term memory, and when I tried to explain it in the interview, much of it had faded.

## I thought I knew, but I did not know it precisely

When Yarn Workspaces and Turborepo came up in the interview, I said Yarn Workspaces would be the better choice. Asked why, I said it lets you unify package dependencies into one.

Next I was asked whether I knew `nodeLinker`. I said it is the setting that ties package dependencies together into one. In fact, `nodeLinker` decides whether installed packages are laid out as a `node_modules` tree or linked through Plug'n'Play. Pinning dependencies to a single version, which is what I was describing, is the job of `resolutions`, and when I was asked about `resolutions`, I said I did not know it. [Yarn linkers](https://yarnpkg.com/features/linkers), [Yarn resolutions](https://yarnpkg.com/configuration/manifest#resolutions)

When I studied this again after the interview, I found that Yarn Workspaces and Turborepo are not alternatives to choose between. Yarn manages dependencies between packages, and Turborepo manages dependencies between tasks. Both `nodeLinker` and `resolutions` are in my own side project repository, yet I could not say precisely what either one does.

## What I am studying now

I studied the two topics I could not explain in the interview again and wrote them up: the dependencies Yarn Workspaces and Turborepo each manage, and how to test infinite scroll and dropdown states one at a time. I am also practicing explaining the topics I prepared before the interview out loud, without looking at my notes.

Where I get stuck explaining is usually where I do not know something precisely. I go back to the docs and the code for those parts, and next time I start explaining from there.

Beyond technology, I keep thinking about how much frontier AI has changed the way we build software, and what that means for how I approach my work and look at products. An agent can implement quickly, but deciding what to build and explaining why is still my job. That was what I was missing in this interview.

I have subscribed to [FE News](https://github.com/naver/fe-news) and FE Article since my first company. More recently, I have been following development trends, including how AI is changing the way we build software, through [GeekNews](https://news.hada.io), [Yozm IT](https://yozm.wishket.com), LinkedIn, X, and Threads. As I read, I compare how other developers think about products and technology with my own work.

The interview showed me how much I still lack, but I enjoy development itself these days. Thanks to AI agents, building costs less and the learning curve for unfamiliar technologies is lower, so it has become easier to catch up quickly in areas I did not know. That makes the time I put in feel more worthwhile. I am also trying many things in side projects. In my two side project services, I [used GraphQL in one and REST in the other](/en/blog/choosing-the-stack-and-boundaries-for-two-side-projects), and [set up the servers and infrastructure myself](/en/blog/why-i-chose-one-ec2-instance-for-my-side-projects). I would like more people to use the products I build and use myself, and I want to keep improving them based on how people respond.

My side projects now are very different from the one I joined on BeSide during my break. Back then I started with a team because I felt I had to do something. Now there is much more one person can do and build. So I want to make things myself, whether that is simply something fun or software that removes a small daily annoyance. I had never set a goal for my side projects, but writing this, I think this has been it.
