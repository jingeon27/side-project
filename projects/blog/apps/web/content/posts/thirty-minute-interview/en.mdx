I could not clearly explain why I had made my own decisions, technologies I had once studied in depth, or the settings of tools I use. This post covers what I did not know and what I am studying now.

I had a 30-minute video interview and could not answer most of the questions well. When I went back over them afterward, the questions were not unusually hard. There was simply a lot I did not know. Even things I thought I knew fell apart once I tried to explain them.

In particular, I could not explain Yarn Workspaces, or how I think about splitting tests and deciding what each one checks. After the interview, I studied both topics again and wrote them up as posts on [Yarn Workspaces and Turborepo](/en/blog/yarn-workspaces-turborepo-build-graph-and-cache) and on [testing infinite scroll and dropdown menus](/en/blog/testing-infinite-scroll-and-dropdown-states).

## I thought this time would be different

At my first company, I built the frontend of a recruiting SaaS. I learned by reading the source of several open source libraries and got to the point of writing library-level type inference and component designs. The CEO, a developer by background, valued my work, and when I said I was leaving, the CEO asked whether I would keep working for the company as a freelancer from home.

After leaving, I burned out a little. I rested for a while, then felt I had to do something, so I joined a team project called Doneness on BeSide. It was a cooking timer that also showed tips for getting food to the doneness you want. About six months into that break, I interviewed with this company for the first time. My development instincts had dulled, and I could not answer most of the questions, including basic syntax questions.

When I joined my current company, I lost trust early on. The approaches from my previous job looked good to me, so I persuaded the team to switch our form and UI libraries. I made the changes before I understood the service's context, which led to a lot of disagreement, and I was not careful enough, so I shipped a lot of bugs. For the next three months, I kept finishing small tasks without mistakes to win that trust back.

Now I am the only frontend developer, building the user-facing service, the hospital admin, and the back office. I introduced testing, monitoring, and a way of working with coding agents on my own. Even after I became the only frontend developer, our pace picked up, and I even heard people on the development team call me the ace.

A year and a half later, I interviewed with the same company again. I had worked hard, and this time I did about three mock interviews and wrote out many expected questions and answers. I thought it would go differently. The questions I got were not the ones I had expected. I do not have the result yet, but the interview itself went the same way as the first one.

## I had not thought the answers through

The questions started from my own work and kept turning into "Why did you do it that way?" They needed real thought, and I had never thought through questions like that in advance.

At work, when I ran into a problem, I looked things up and read the code as far as I needed, then decided. I rarely went back to work out why the decision was right or how it compared with other options. So in the interview I had to build answers on the spot, and words came out while I was still organizing my thoughts. Even as I spoke, I was not sure I was answering the question.

Testing was the same. At work I had kept tests for a new feature's expected behavior separate from tests that reproduce bugs found in production, but when asked why I split them that way, I could not explain it in my own words.

## I did the work, but could not remember why

I had already noticed that I could not remember my reasons while preparing. When I asked myself why I chose the file upload approach in a side project, or why my two services used different API styles, I could not answer right away. The code and docs recorded what I changed and what I checked. For many decisions, though, I had not written down what I compared or why I chose one option over the other.

While working with coding agents, I kept writing docs and rules so the next task could start from the same context. Those notes were for the agent to keep working. They were not quite the reasons I would need to explain a decision to someone later. The results were recorded, but the reasons lived only in my memory, and that memory faded faster than I expected.

Technologies were no different. Some I had studied in depth and some only as far as I needed, and studying something in depth did not mean it stayed with me. I looked into monorepos quite thoroughly when I first introduced one. That knowledge never made it into long-term memory, and when I tried to explain it in the interview, much of it had faded.

## I thought I knew, but I did not know it precisely

When Yarn Workspaces and Turborepo came up in the interview, I said Yarn Workspaces would be the better choice. Asked why, I said it lets you unify package dependencies into one.

Next I was asked whether I knew `nodeLinker`. I said it is the setting that ties package dependencies together into one. In fact, `nodeLinker` decides whether installed packages are laid out as a `node_modules` tree or linked through Plug'n'Play. Pinning dependencies to a single version, which is what I was describing, is the job of `resolutions`, and when I was asked about `resolutions`, I said I did not know it.

When I studied this again after the interview, I found that Yarn Workspaces and Turborepo are not alternatives to choose between. Yarn manages dependencies between packages, and Turborepo manages dependencies between tasks. Both `nodeLinker` and `resolutions` are in my own side project repository, yet I could not say precisely what either one does.

## What I am studying now

I am practicing explaining the topics I prepared for the interview out loud, in under a minute, without looking at my notes.

- Browser rendering: the order from the DOM and CSSOM through style calculation, layout, paint, rasterization, and compositing, and which step a given change sends the browser back to
- The event loop: when tasks, microtasks, and `requestAnimationFrame` callbacks run, and why long tasks block input and screen updates
- The browser back button and the History API, React's render and commit phases, cookies and CORS
- Topics I studied again after the interview: the dependencies Yarn Workspaces and Turborepo each manage, and how to test infinite scroll and dropdown states one at a time

The point where an explanation stalls is the point I do not know precisely. When I get stuck, I go back to the docs and the code, and next time I start explaining from that point.

Beyond specific technologies, I am thinking again about a few things: how much frontier AI has changed the way we build software, what attitude I should bring to my work as a result, and how I should look at products. An agent can implement quickly, but deciding what to build and explaining why is still my job. That was exactly what I was missing in this interview.

So I subscribe to GeekNews, FE Article, and FE News. I follow how AI is changing development and how other developers think about products and technology, and I hold that up against my own work.

The interview showed me how much I still lack, but I enjoy development itself these days. Building has become cheaper, so picking up an unfamiliar technology feels lighter, and I am trying many things in side projects. In the two services I built as side projects, I used GraphQL in one and REST in the other, and set up the servers and infrastructure myself. I would like more people to use the products I build and use myself, and I want to keep improving them based on how people respond.

My side projects now are very different from the one I joined on BeSide during my break. Back then I started with a team because I felt I had to do something. Now there is much more one person can do and build. So I want to make things myself, whether that is simply something fun or software that removes a small daily annoyance. I had never set a goal for my side projects, but writing this post made me realize that this is the goal.
